CIS137 · 3.1
AI as a research partner

A fast research partner. A poor final source.

lead
A pointer to where an answer might be found.
primary source
The organization or document where a fact starts.
citation
Publisher, title, date, location: enough for someone to find it.

Leadthe county health department probably publishes heat data

Factthe county reported this number in this report

CIS137 · 3.1
Three tools, three jobs

Pick the tool by what you need next.

What do you need next?

The page itself Search engine

Gives: a list of pages that match your words

You still: pick the pages, judge who wrote them, read them

Ideas, angles, search terms, or a summary of text you supply Chat assistant

Gives: a written answer with no guaranteed source

You still: treat every claim as a lead and find its source

A first pass with sources attached Research tool

Gives: a written answer with citations you can open

You still: open each citation and confirm it says what the answer claims

A link shown in a chat is still a lead. Open it.

CIS137 · 3.1
Quick Check 3.1

The bulletin ends with an exception. Will it survive?

A manager asks for a two-sentence summary of a safety bulletin that ends with "These steps do not apply to units built before 2010." Write the one sentence you would add to the prompt, and explain what it prevents.

Add: “The summary must keep the caveat that these steps do not apply to units built before 2010.” It prevents a summary that keeps the steps and drops the exception, so readers apply them to every unit. Unless you name it, the model chooses what to drop.

CIS137 · 3.2
Creative work: images

“No text” is a request, not a guarantee.

Image promptA flat illustration of two volunteers fixing a bicycle in a sunny church parking lot, warm desert colors, no text, no logos, no recognizable faces.

  1. Text in the picture. Letters that look like words and are not.
  2. People who look real. Prefer no recognizable faces.
  3. Logos and brands. A sponsor you do not have.
  4. Accuracy to the event. Count bikes, tools, people.
  5. Alt text. You write it. The generator does not.

Quick Check 3.2

The blurb says it is 150 words. Is it?

A tool returns a blurb that says "(Word count: 150)." Describe the one check you run before you trust that line, and what you do if it fails.

Count the words yourself with a word counter. The tool’s count is a claim, not a check. If your count differs, the line is wrong: fix the length, then count again before the blurb goes out.

CIS137 · 3.3
What works instead

Ask for evidence, not a verdict.

  • Evidence of processDrafts, notes, version history, sources, a prompt log.
  • DisclosureNot “can we catch it?” but “did they tell us?”
  • Content credentialsHelp when present. A missing one proves nothing.
CIS137 · 3.3
Quick Check 3.3

The detector says 85 percent. Is that proof?

An instructor's detector flags a student essay as 85 percent likely AI-generated. Using the 2023 studies, explain two reasons that score is not proof, and name the evidence you would ask the student for instead.

The tools are inaccurate: all 14 tested scored below 80 percent accuracy. They are biased: more than half of 91 essays by non-native English writers were flagged. Ask for evidence of process instead: drafts, notes, version history, sources, or a prompt log.
CIS137 · 3.4
Five principles

Five principles, five questions before you send.

  1. 1HonestyDoes my reader know AI helped?
  2. 2AttributionDid I credit every source, and did I check that each one says what I claim?
  3. 3ConsentDid everyone whose information or likeness appears agree to this use?
  4. 4PrivacyDid anything go into the tool that I would not post in public?
  5. 5HarmCould this mislead, embarrass, or hurt someone, and who carries that risk?

Who owns it? With current technology, prompts alone do not make you the author (US Copyright Office guidance, January 2025).

CIS137 · 3.4
The disclosure statement

A disclosure says three things and adds nothing.

First try · full note Sample, Oct 2026. Your output will differ.

[...] we used an AI chat assistant to help brainstorm possible names and to write a first draft of the menu description. [...] The name was chosen by the owner. The description was rewritten in our own words, and every ingredient listed was checked against our actual recipe. What you read here, and what ends up in your taco, is what we make by hand in our kitchen.

Questions about ingredients or allergens? Ask us at the truck or get in touch through our contact page. [...]

Tightened · 33 words Sample, Oct 2026. Your output will differ.

Use two sentences and under 35 words. Say what the AI did, what I did, and what I checked. Do not claim anything I did not tell you.

I used an AI chat assistant to brainstorm names for this taco and draft its first menu description. I chose the name, rewrote the description myself, and checked every ingredient against my recipe.

Quick Check 3.4

An angry client email. What goes in?

A coworker wants to paste a client's angry email, including the client's name and account number, into a chat assistant to draft a calm reply. Apply the privacy check and list what goes in and what stays out.

Goes in: the task (draft a calm reply) and bracket labels such as [Client] and [Account]. Stays out: the client’s name and the account number. Write both columns before anything is pasted.
